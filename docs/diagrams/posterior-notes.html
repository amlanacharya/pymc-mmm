Posterior Notes

Every model family in pymc-marketing, read two ways at once: the code path a fit() call actually takes, and the equation that path is computing. Diagrams in teal show flow; cards in amber show the math underneath it — each pinned to the file and line it comes from.

Foundation

One base class, five model families

MMM, CLV, Customer Choice, Bass, and PIE all extend ModelBuilder or RegressionModelBuilder. The base class owns nothing mathematical — it owns state: has a model been built, has it been fit, has it been saved. Every subsystem below plugs its own likelihood into the same four-step machine.

model_builder.py — load() checks a stored model id/version before rebuilding, raising DifferentModelError on mismatch. mlflow.py:1365-1472 autologs RV counts and divergences on every fit().
Media Mix Model

Spend → contribution → posterior

Each channel's raw spend is scaled, reshaped by adstock (carryover) then saturation (diminishing returns), and summed with a seasonal term into one likelihood. A lift test, when available, doesn't feed the likelihood directly — it calibrates the saturation curve that already sits there.

Nodes in amber are where a formula, not just a data reshape, does the work — those get their own card below.

Adstock and saturation

Two transforms, applied per channel, that turn a spend series into a shape the likelihood can use: one for memory, one for decreasing returns.

Geometric adstock — carryover

transformers.py:319
\[ w_t = \alpha^{t}, \qquad x^{*}_{t} = \sum_{l=0}^{L} w_l \, x_{t-l} \]
Media effect doesn't stop the day spend stops. Each lag l back gets weight αl, so a bigger α (closer to 1) means the channel's effect decays slowly — a billboard, not a flash sale.

Logistic saturation — diminishing returns

transformers.py:582
\[ f(x) = \frac{1 - e^{-\lambda x}}{1 + e^{-\lambda x}} \]
Doubling spend rarely doubles response. λ controls how fast the curve bends over — a large λ saturates a channel at low spend, meaning more budget there buys almost nothing extra.

Seasonality and time-varying effects

The likelihood's additive term outside the media channels — calendar cycles plus anything drifting slowly over time.

Fourier seasonality

fourier.py:313
\[ s(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \Big[ a_k \sin\!\big(2\pi k t / P\big) + b_k \cos\!\big(2\pi k t / P\big) \Big] \]
Weekly and yearly cycles as a sum of waves. K terms buys smoother or sharper seasonal shape; the coefficients ak, bk are fit like any other parameter.

HSGP — time-varying trend

hsgp.py:244
\[ f(t) \approx \sum_{j=1}^{M} \phi_j(t)\, \sqrt{S(\sqrt{\lambda_j})}\; \beta_j, \qquad \beta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1) \]
A full Gaussian Process is expensive to sample. The Hilbert-Space approximation swaps it for a fixed basis φj weighted by M ordinary Normal parameters — same smooth-drift behavior, PyMC-cheap to fit.

Calibration and optimization

Two places real-world ground truth enters after the curves are defined: an experiment tells the model its saturation curve is right, and an optimizer uses the fitted curves to answer "where should next quarter's budget go."

Lift-test calibration

lift_test.py:437
\[ \text{Potential} \;=\; \log \, \mathrm{Gamma}\big(\Delta_{\text{observed}} \,\big|\, \Delta_{\text{model}}(x)\big) \]
An incrementality test measures a real lift Δobserved for a spend change. A Gamma potential adds to the log-posterior whenever the model's own saturation curve predicts a different lift — nudging the curve toward what the experiment actually saw.

Budget optimizer

budget_optimizer.py:1548
\[ \max_{b_1,\dots,b_C} \; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{c=1}^{C} f_c\big(\text{adstock}_c(b_c)\big)\right] \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_c b_c \le B \]
With adstock and saturation curves fixed from the posterior, this is now an ordinary constrained optimization — scipy's SLSQP finds the per-channel budgets bc that maximize total expected response under a total budget B.
Customer Lifetime Value

Same RFM data, three questions

Every CLV model here reads the same summary per customer — frequency x, recency t_x, observation window T — and answers a different question: will they come back, when will they stop, and how much will they spend.

clv/distributions.py holds the shared custom log-likelihoods that BetaGeo and Pareto/NBD both compile against.

Purchase timing and monetary value

Two non-contractual timing models that differ only in when a customer is allowed to churn, plus a spend model that is independent of timing entirely.

BetaGeo/NBD — dropout each period

distributions.py:478-491
α ~ Weibull(2, 10) r ~ Weibull(2, 1) a = φκ, b = (1−φ)κ φ ~ Uniform(0,1), κ ~ Pareto(1,1)
\[ L(r,\alpha,a,b \mid x,t_x,T) = \frac{\Gamma(r+x)}{\Gamma(r)}\frac{\alpha^{r}}{(\alpha+t_x)^{r+x}} \;+\; \mathbb{1}_{x>0}\,\frac{\Gamma(a+b)\Gamma(b+x)}{\Gamma(b)\Gamma(a+b+x)}\left[\frac{a}{b+x-1}\right]\left(\frac{\alpha+t_x}{\alpha+T}\right)^{r+x} \]
A customer can only drop out right after a purchase. a, b parameterize a Beta prior on that per-transaction dropout probability; r, α parameterize a Gamma prior on purchase rate. beta_geo.py:169-178

Pareto/NBD — dropout any time

distributions.py:159-174
r, s ~ Weibull(2, 1) α, β ~ Weibull(2, 10)
\[ L(r,\alpha,s,\beta \mid x,t_x,T) \;\propto\; \frac{\Gamma(r+x)\,\alpha^{r}\beta^{s}}{ \left(\alpha+T\right)^{r+x}\left(\beta+T\right)^{s}} \Big[\,1 + \tfrac{s}{r+s+x}\; {}_2F_1\big(r+s+x,\,s+1;\,r+s+x+1;\,z\big)\Big] \]
Letting dropout happen at any continuous instant — not just after an order — pulls in the Gauss hypergeometric function ₂F₁. pymc-marketing evaluates it in log-space to keep it numerically stable at the parameter ranges MCMC actually explores.

Gamma-Gamma — monetary value

gamma_gamma.py:363-373
p, q ~ Weibull(2, 1) v ~ Weibull(2, 10)
\[ \mathbb{E}[M \mid p,q,v,x,\bar z] = \frac{\Gamma(px+q)}{\Gamma(px)\,\Gamma(q)}\; v^{q}\,\bar z^{\,px-1}\, x^{px}\, \big(x\bar z + v\big)^{-(px+q)} \]
Deliberately has no time in it at all — spend-per-transaction z̄ is modeled as independent of how often or how long a customer buys, which is the standard (and testable) Gamma-Gamma assumption.
Discrete Choice

Aggregate shares vs. individual choices

Same underlying idea — a utility per product, converted to a probability — applied at two different resolutions: market shares you can only observe in aggregate, and individual respondents whose exact choice you saw.

Utility and share equations

BayesianBLP — aggregate market shares

bayesian_blp.py:741-880
\[ \delta_{jm} = \alpha p_{jm} + \sum_k \beta_k x_{jmk} + \xi_{jm}, \qquad s_j = \mathrm{softmax}(\delta)_j \]
\[ \log(s_j/s_0) \sim \mathcal{N}\big(\log(\hat s_j/\hat s_0),\, \sigma^2\big) \]
You never see individual buyers here, only the market share each product landed at — ξjm is an unobserved product/market shock, and the likelihood matches log-share-ratios rather than raw shares for numerical reasons.

MixedLogit — individual random coefficients

mixed_logit.py:687-982
\[ U_{ij} = \sum_d X_{jd}\,\beta_{id} + W_i \delta + \alpha_j, \qquad \beta_{id} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_d, \sigma_d) \]
\[ P_{ij} = \mathrm{softmax}(U_i)_j, \qquad y_i \sim \mathrm{Categorical}(P_i) \]
Each respondent i gets their own taste coefficients βid, drawn from a population Normal — this is what separates "mixed" logit from a plain multinomial logit with one shared β.

MaxDiff — best-worst scaling

maxdiff.py:901-926
\[ P(\text{best}) = \mathrm{softmax}(U), \qquad P(\text{worst}) = \mathrm{softmax}(-U_{\setminus \text{best}}) \]
Two Categorical likelihoods off the same utility vector — picking the best item and, separately, the worst among what's left — extracts more signal per question than a single choice would.

MVITS — cannibalization

mv_its.py:154-332
β ~ Dirichlet(0.5)
\[ \mu_{it} = \text{intercept}_i - y_t\,\beta_i, \qquad y_{it} \sim \mathrm{TruncatedNormal}(\mu_{it}, \cdot\,;\, y\ge 0) \]
A launched product's sales yt pull down existing products' expected sales through βi — a Dirichlet prior keeps the cannibalized share split across existing products summing sensibly, and pm.do() runs the no-launch counterfactual.
